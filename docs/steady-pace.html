What a real engineering day looks like, and what to say when work gets hard.

A good day is 2–3 hours of deep focus.

Not eight. That is what studies of real engineers keep finding. If you have been feeling that you do too little, you may be comparing yourself with a day nobody actually has.

    Total hours and limits

    • A normal week. 51.7% of developers worked 40–44 hours, in older Stack Overflow data reported in 2019.
    • The health line. Working 55 or more hours a week carries an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease, compared with 35–40 hours (WHO and ILO, 2021).
    • The focus ceiling. Elite performers reportedly cap hard practice at about 4 hours a day. This comes from summaries of research on expert practice; the original paper was not read for this page.
    • Indian IT. In a 2025 Blind survey, 72% regularly exceed 48 hours, nearly 1 in 4 work more than 70, and 83% report burnout. Its 1,450 respondents chose to answer, so it is not a national figure. Common does not mean healthy, and you do not have to match it.

    Most of this data is from 2022 or earlier, before daily AI coding tools, so the coding figure may have changed. Nothing newer turned up when this page was written in November 2026.

    Your daily targets

    These are judgment calls based on the numbers above. No study sets these exact figures.

    Time at work
    8–9 hours a day, 40–45 a week.
    Deep focus
    2–3 hours is a good day. 4 is a great day. There is no need to aim higher.
    Coding
    1–2 hours is normal. You are not behind.
    Hard limit
    55 hours a week is the line. A rare crunch happens, but it should never become the plan.
    After work
    Studying and side projects use the same mental energy, so count them inside your 4 hours.

    A simple shape for the day

    1. Protect one 90-minute block in your best hours, before messages take over.
    2. Put reviews, replies and meetings in the hours when your energy dips.
    3. When you have read the same line three times, your focus is spent. Take a walk or switch to something light.

    Before you close the laptop

    • I moved one important thing forward.
    • Anyone who needs to hear something from me has been told.
    • I wrote down where to start tomorrow.

    All three? You are done for today. The rest will still be there tomorrow, and you will be better at it after a real rest.

    None of this means you should only work or log four hours. Meetings, reviews and reading are real work, and your timesheet should show the hours you actually worked.

    Guilt check

    When one of these thoughts shows up, here is the other side of it.

    You were hired after someone checked your work and chose you. You do not have to earn the job again every day.

    Protect yourself quietly

    Small habits that mean you rarely need to defend yourself at all.

    Write it down after you talk

    After a decision made on a call or at a desk, send two lines confirming it. It is normal, and it protects everyone if memories differ later.

    Keep a wins note

    Every Friday, write three lines: what you shipped, who you helped, what you learned. At appraisal time you will have a year of evidence. Keep confidential company details out of it.

    Give status before you are asked

    One clear line a day means nobody has to wonder. People who are not wondering do not hover.

    Raise risks early, in writing

    If a date looks shaky, say so the day you know. If it goes wrong later, the note shows you flagged it.

    Get work into the tracker

    A ticket beats a chat message, and a chat message beats a hallway ask. Work in the tracker is work people can see you did.

    Keep "let me check" ready

    You never have to answer on the spot. "Let me check and come back by 3" is a complete, professional answer.

    These habits are for being clear, never for blaming people or covering up a mistake. If you got something wrong, say so early. That protects you too.

    When it is more than a tough week

    Everyone has hard weeks, and a hard week is not burnout. WHO describes burn-out as the result of chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, and lists three signs:

    • Exhaustion. You feel drained, and rest does not bring you back.
    • Distance. You feel negative or cynical about your job, or keep pulling away from it.
    • Getting less done. You feel less effective at work than you used to be.

    WHO classes it as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. That is worth remembering: it comes from the load and the conditions, not from something wrong with you.

    What helps

    1. Use the "overloaded" lines in What to say to bring the load down.
    2. Take real time off, with work notifications silenced.
    3. See a doctor if poor sleep, low mood or physical symptoms last more than a couple of weeks.
    4. In India, Tele-MANAS is a free, 24/7 government mental health helpline, available in 20 languages: 14416 or 1800-89-14416.
